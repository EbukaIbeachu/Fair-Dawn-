THE FAIR DAWN
A short story


PART ONE: THE ANNOUNCEMENT

The serum came the way most terrible things come now, on a Tuesday, during an advertisement break.

Chidinma was marking Primary Five essays at her kitchen table in Surulere when the generator coughed and the television returned to life mid-sentence. A woman in a white suit stood before a white wall, smiling the smile of someone who had been paid well to smile.

"…a new dawn for human equality," she was saying. "Lumina is safe, permanent, and painless. One course of three injections. Clinically proven across forty thousand participants. Lumina does not change who you are. It only changes how the world sees you."

Then the before-and-after photographs. A man from Accra, dark as polished mahogany, became a man with skin the colour of skimmed milk. His nose was the same. His eyes were the same. His smile was the same, but somehow it was not.

Chidinma put her red pen down.

She assumed, at first, that it was one of those Instagram creams that her students' mothers bought in Balogun Market, the ones that left women's knuckles grey and their faces peeling. She assumed it would be banned within the month.

It was not banned. Within the month it was law.

The Geneva Accord on Harmonised Identity was signed by one hundred and twelve nations in a single afternoon. The language was gentle and very carefully chosen. Nobody was required to take Lumina. Nobody would be punished for refusing it. But those who completed the course would be issued a Clear Card, and the Clear Card would come with what the documents called facilitations.

Visa-free travel to the forty signatory economies. Priority consideration for public-sector employment. Mortgage rates two points below market. Fast-track university admission abroad. Access to the new Harmonised Health Scheme, which covered things the old schemes never had.

"It is not about colour," said the Minister of Interior on Channels Television that night, leaning back in a leather chair. "It is about removing friction. For centuries our people have been stopped at borders, refused loans, followed in shops, overlooked for promotion. Now the world is offering us a key. Are we too proud to use a key?"

The Nigerian government signed in the second week. Ghana signed in the third. South Africa held out for four months, through protests that filled the Union Buildings lawn, and then signed too.

By the time the rains came, there were Lumina clinics in every state capital, clean white buildings with clean white signs, and the queues outside them began at four in the morning.


PART TWO: TOBENNA

Her brother Tobenna was the first in the family to say it out loud.

He said it on a Sunday, over pepper soup at their grandmother's house in Ikeja, with the ceiling fan turning slow circles above them. He said it the way you announce you have bought a car you cannot afford: quickly, looking at the bowl.

"I've booked my first appointment."

Nobody asked for what. Everybody knew for what.

Tobenna was thirty-one. He had a first-class degree in petroleum engineering from UNILAG and a master's from Aberdeen that had cost their late father a plot of land in Nnewi. He had applied for two hundred and six jobs in four countries. He kept a spreadsheet. Chidinma had seen it once: a column for the company, a column for the date, and a column titled Reason Given, which was almost always empty.

"Last month I interviewed with a firm in Houston," he said. "Video call. The panel loved me. Second round, loved me. Final round, the HR woman asked if I had considered the Accord. Considered. Like it was a pension plan." He laughed without any laughter in it. "They hired a Clear Card guy from Kumasi. Less experience. Two years less."

"So they are blackmailing you," said Chidinma.

"They are offering me a door, Chi. You want me to stand outside it for principle? Principle doesn't pay Mummy's dialysis."

That was the thing about Tobenna. He was never cruel, and he was rarely wrong about money. He sent home more than anyone. He had paid Chidinma's final-year fees without being asked. He was the one who drove three hours to Abeokuta when their mother's kidneys first failed, and the one who still sat with her through every four-hour session at the clinic, reading her the football results.

"It's skin, Chi," he said, softer now. "It's just skin. I'll still be me. I'll still be Igbo. I'll still speak the language. I'll still come home every Christmas and eat Nne's ofe onugbu until I can't breathe."

At the head of the table, their grandmother said nothing at all. She only looked at her grandson for a long moment, as though she were memorising his face.


PART THREE: THE ARGUMENTS

The country argued about Lumina the way it argued about everything: loudly, everywhere, and all at once.

On the radio in the danfo buses, callers shouted over each other. In the beer parlours, men slammed Star bottles on plastic tables. On Twitter the hashtags multiplied faster than anyone could read them: #ClearAndFree, #NotForSale, #MyMelaninMyMatter, #JustSkin.

A famous Afrobeats star took the serum and posted a video of himself in Dubai, arms wide on a yacht. "They used to search my bag at every airport," he wrote. "Now they carry it for me." Eleven million likes.

A professor of history at Nsukka wrote an essay that was shared almost as widely. She called Lumina "the most efficient colonial instrument ever designed, because it requires no soldiers. It asks us to hold the whip ourselves and calls it freedom." She pointed out that the Accord said nothing about changing the borders, the banks, or the minds of the people who had built the friction. It asked only that the people who suffered the friction remove themselves from it. "They did not fix the lock," she wrote. "They asked us to change the shape of our hands."

The pastors split down the middle. One preached that God made no mistakes in the womb. Another preached that Joseph wore Egyptian robes to save his family, and was not condemned for it.

The traditional rulers were mostly silent, which people understood to mean they had been paid, or that they were afraid, or both.

Chidinma's own staff room was a small war. Mr Adeyemi, who taught mathematics and had four children, said simply: "I cannot eat culture. My children cannot eat culture. If this card opens a door for them, I will take it and I will apologise to my ancestors later."

Mrs Bassey, the head of English, slammed down her tea. "And what do you think your ancestors will say? That you were practical? Our grandparents survived slave ships and the colonial office and three coups without bleaching themselves out of existence. And we will do it for a mortgage discount?"

"Our grandparents didn't have a choice," Adeyemi said quietly. "We do. That is the cruelty of it. They have made it a choice, so that when it is done, they can say we chose."

Chidinma said nothing in the staff room. But that night she lay awake, turning his words over like a coin. They have made it a choice. She understood, finally, why it was not a law that forced it. Force would have been resisted. Force would have been a crime. This was something slower and much harder to fight. This was an incentive.


PART FOUR: NNE'S ROOM

Their grandmother's room smelled of camphor, palm kernel oil, and old paper.

Nne was eighty-four. She had been a midwife in Onitsha for forty years and had delivered, by her own count, three thousand two hundred babies, including half the members of the current state assembly. She still kept a ledger of every one: name, date, weight, the time of the first cry.

Chidinma went to her the week after Tobenna's announcement. She did not know what she wanted. Perhaps permission to be angry. Perhaps permission not to be.

Nne was sitting on her bed with a small calabash of uli dye on her knee, black and thick as ink, made from the crushed seeds of the uli plant the way her own mother had taught her.

"Give me your hand," she said.

Chidinma held it out. Nne began to draw on the back of it with a thin stick: a spiral, then a line of small triangles, then a curving shape like a bird's wing.

"This one is agwolagwo," Nne said, tapping the spiral. "The coiled snake. It means the circle that does not end. This one is onwa, the moon. This one has no name in English. My mother called it 'the thing that remembers.'"

"Nne, why are you doing this?"

"Because uli is drawn on dark skin." The old woman did not look up. "It was made for us. The dye is dark, and the skin is dark, and the beauty is in how the two speak to each other. On white skin it looks like dirt. Like a bruise. Like a mistake." She finished the bird's wing and put down the stick. "When I was a girl, the missionaries told us uli was heathen. They told us our names were heathen. They gave us English names at baptism and Latin names at school. My mother hid her calabash in the yam barn. Because of her, I can still draw this. Because of me, you can still wear it."

She took Chidinma's painted hand in both of hers.

"Your brother is a good boy. He is not wrong that he is hungry. Hunger is not a sin. But listen to me, nwa m. Every time they come for us, they come with something that looks like a gift. The missionaries came with schools. The traders came with mirrors and gin. Now they come with a card."

"So what do I tell him?"

"Tell him nothing. He has already decided." Nne's eyes were wet but her voice was steady. "You, I am telling. Someone must remember what we looked like. Someone must keep the calabash in the yam barn."


PART FIVE: THE CLINIC

Chidinma went with Tobenna to his third injection, because he asked her to, and because he was her brother.

The Lumina clinic on Awolowo Road had been a bank. The marble floors remained, and the high ceiling, but the walls had been painted a white so bright it hurt. Soft music played, something with flutes. Young nurses in white uniforms moved between rows of white chairs, and every one of the nurses had completed the course. Their skin was pale and even and strangely without shine, like paper.

Tobenna was already lighter. After the first two injections his face had faded to the colour of weak tea. His palms and the soles of his feet, which had always been pale, now matched the rest of him. Only his lips held a little of their old darkness, and the nurse said that would go with the third dose.

In the waiting area Chidinma watched the others. A market woman with her two teenage daughters, all three holding hands. A young man in a suit with a laptop open, answering emails. An elderly couple who sat very straight and did not speak. A mother with a baby on her back, filling a form for herself, and then, after hesitating, turning the page to the section marked Dependants.

On the wall a large screen played testimonials on a loop.

I got the job the same week.

The police don't stop me any more.

My daughter's teachers finally see her.

I feel free.

Chidinma noticed that nobody in the videos ever said I feel like myself.

When the nurse called Tobenna's name, he stood and squeezed her shoulder. "Wait for me," he said, the way he used to when they were children walking to school and he ran ahead to buy puff-puff.

She waited. Forty minutes later he came out holding a small white card, laminated, with a hologram of a sunrise. His face in the photo was the face she had known all her life, and it was also a stranger's.

"It's done," he said. He turned the card over in his fingers, and for a moment he did not look triumphant. He looked like a man checking his pockets for something he had already lost.

Then his phone buzzed. He read the message, and his whole body changed.

"Houston," he whispered. "They want me to start in March."


PART SIX: THE CHILD

Nne died in the second harmattan after the Accord, quietly, in her sleep, with her ledger on the bedside table.

At the funeral in Onitsha, Chidinma counted. Of the forty-one members of the extended family who came, twenty-six were Clear. Her cousins from Abuja. Her aunties from London. Uncle Emeka, who had been a chief, and who now looked like a retired Portuguese sailor in an agbada.

Chidinma had brought Nne's calabash. Before the burial she sat on a bench in the compound and painted uli on the hands of anyone who asked, the way Nne would have done. The children came first, the dark ones, giggling. Then, slowly, some of the adults.

On the dark hands the patterns sang. On the pale hands they looked exactly as Nne had said they would: like bruises, like dirt, like something gone wrong. One of her cousins looked at her painted wrist and began to cry, and could not explain why, and went to wash it off.

Tobenna could not come. His new employer had a policy about unscheduled travel in the first two years, and he was on a project in the Gulf of Mexico. He sent money for the burial. He sent a long voice note that Chidinma listened to three times. He sent photographs of his new wife, Ifeoma, a Clear nurse from Enugu he had met in Houston, glowing and enormously pregnant.

The baby was born in April, in a hospital in Texas, and the first message Chidinma received was not a photograph. It was a single line from her brother at three in the morning, Lagos time.

Chi, she is dark.

Then, a minute later: Nobody told us.

It was in the documents, of course. Page forty-seven, clause nine, in the small grey print that nobody reads: "Lumina is a somatic treatment and does not alter the germline. Offspring of treated individuals will express their inherited phenotype." The serum changed the skin. It did not change the blood, or the bones, or the long inheritance written in every cell. The children would always come back.

And the Accord had planned for that. There was a provision, introduced in the second year with very little news coverage, called the Family Continuity Protocol. To keep the family's Clear benefits whole, including the parents' visa category, the child would need to begin a paediatric course at six months. "Gentle, age-appropriate dosing," the brochure said. "So your family can share one future."

Tobenna called her properly the next evening. He sounded exhausted. In the background she could hear the baby, a thin, furious cry.

"The paediatrician brought it up before Ifeoma had even stitched," he said. "Before. Like a vaccination schedule. And I just stood there holding her, Chi. I held her and I looked at her, and she looked like you. She looked like Mummy. She looked like Nne." His voice broke. "I didn't know what my own face looked like any more until I saw hers."


PART SEVEN: WHAT REMAINS

They came home for Christmas when the baby was five months old.

Chidinma met them at Murtala Muhammed Airport. She saw them before they saw her, two pale figures pushing a trolley through the crowd, and in Ifeoma's arms a small dark bundle in a yellow knitted hat. At passport control, she noticed, the officer had waved the parents through the Clear lane with a smile. Then he had looked at the baby, and at her green Nigerian passport, and directed Ifeoma to the other queue.

They had named her Nkechi Uloma. Nkechi, what God gave. Uloma, a good home.

That night, after their mother had gone to bed, still weak from dialysis but laughing more than she had in years, Tobenna found Chidinma in Nne's old room. The house in Ikeja was Chidinma's now. She had kept the room exactly as it was: the camphor, the palm kernel oil, the ledger, the calabash.

He sat on the edge of the bed with his daughter asleep on his chest, and for a long time neither of them spoke.

"The appointment is booked for January," he said finally. "Houston. If we don't go, Ifeoma loses her visa category. Maybe I do too. The company is very understanding, they keep saying, very understanding. That's how you know."

"And what do you want?"

"I want what I wanted before. I want her to have every door." He looked down at the baby. "But I keep thinking about what Nne said. That they always come with something that looks like a gift. I thought she was being old-fashioned. I thought I was being smart." He laughed softly. "I am the first man in our family in four hundred years who can walk into any bank in the world. And I can't look at my own daughter without feeling like I owe her an apology."

Chidinma did not tell him what to do. She had learned that from Nne too.

Instead she reached for the calabash. The dye inside was fresh; she made a new batch every month, the way she had been taught. She took the thin stick, and very gently, so as not to wake her, she lifted Nkechi's tiny hand.

On the back of it, against that dark, soft skin, she drew a coiled snake. Then the moon. Then the curving shape like a bird's wing, the one with no name in English.

The patterns sang.

Tobenna watched. His own hands lay pale on his knees, and he turned one over and looked at it for a long time, as though trying to remember something.

"What's that last one?" he asked.

"Nne's mother called it the thing that remembers."

Outside, Lagos roared on, generators and horns and someone's party music drifting over the walls. On a billboard on the expressway, lit all night, a smiling family in white stood before a white sunrise beneath the words: ONE WORLD. ONE FUTURE. LUMINA.

In Nne's room, the baby stirred, opened her eyes, and grasped her father's pale finger with her small dark hand. She held on with the fierce, astonishing strength of the newly born, and she did not let go.


THE END
