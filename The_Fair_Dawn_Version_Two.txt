The Fair Dawn: Version Two

A longer telling, with more voices.

Part One: The Announcement

Before you accept a gift, ask what it costs the giver. Then ask what it costs you. — one of Nne's sayings

The serum came the way most terrible things come now, on a Tuesday, during an advertisement break.

Chidinma Okafor was marking Primary Five essays at her kitchen table in Surulere when the generator coughed and the television came back to life mid-sentence. A woman in a white suit stood before a white wall, smiling the smile of someone who had been paid well to smile.

"…a new dawn for human equality," she was saying. "Lumina is safe, permanent and painless. One course of three injections. Clinically proven across forty thousand participants. Lumina does not change who you are. It only changes how the world sees you."

Then the before-and-after photographs. A man from Accra, dark as polished mahogany, became a man with skin the colour of skimmed milk. His nose was the same. His eyes were the same. His smile was the same, and somehow it was not.

Her phone buzzed. Obinna, her fiancé: Are you watching Channels? This thing is mad.

A minute later: Mad but interesting o.

She looked at the second message much longer than the first.

---

The next morning her pupils had already heard.

"Miss," said Tunde from the front row, hand high, "my daddy said by next year everybody in Nigeria will be white. Is it true?"

"It is not for everybody," said Zainab, with the authority of a girl whose mother read newspapers. "It is only for people who want to travel."

From the back, Chukwudi folded his arms. "I don't want to be white. White people cannot dance."

The class exploded with laughter. Chidinma laughed too, until she saw Halima, the quiet one by the window, who had not laughed at all.

"Miss," Halima said softly, "if I become white, will my grandmother still know me?"

The room went still. Thirty-two small faces turned to the teacher, waiting for the adult answer.

Chidinma opened her mouth and found nothing in it. "Open your mathematics books," she said at last. "Page forty." She hated herself a little for the rest of the day.

---

She assumed it was one of those creams sold in Balogun Market, the ones that left women's knuckles grey and their faces peeling. She assumed it would be banned within the month.

It was not banned. Within the month it was law.

The Geneva Accord on Harmonised Identity was signed by one hundred and twelve nations in a single afternoon. Nobody was required to take Lumina. Nobody would be punished for refusing it. But those who completed the course would receive a Clear Card, and the Clear Card came with what the documents called facilitations: visa-free travel to forty economies, priority for public-sector jobs, mortgages two points below market, fast-track admission to foreign universities, and a health scheme that covered what the old ones never had.

That night the Minister of Interior sat on Channels Television, leaning back in a leather chair, opposite a young journalist named Funmi Adeoye who did not lean back at all.

"Honourable Minister," she said, "if the problem is that the world discriminates against Black people, why is the solution that Black people stop being Black?"

"Madam, I am a practical man." He spread his hands. "You can wait for the world's heart to change. Your grandchildren can wait. Or you can walk through the door today."

"And who built the door, sir?"

He smiled. "Does it matter who built it, if it opens?"

"It matters," said Funmi, "if they also built the wall."

The Minister's smile stayed exactly where it was. Two weeks later Funmi Adeoye's programme was moved to a slot after midnight.

---

On City Voice 101.9, the most listened-to call-in show in Lagos belonged to Segun Oyelaran, a big-voiced man with albinism who wore wide hats and dark glasses against the sun and called every caller "my dear".

"My dear people," he said that Friday, "I need to tell you something before you start phoning in. I have been the colour they are selling my whole life. Nobody gave me a card. In primary school they called me afin and would not sit beside me. In my village a man once offered my mother money for my hair. My skin burns in the Lagos sun like paper in a fire." He paused, and the whole city seemed to pause with him. "So when they tell you white skin is a key, my dear, ask me about the lock."

The phone lines jammed for three hours.

---

Nigeria signed in the second week. Ghana signed in the third. South Africa held out for four months, through protests that filled the Union Buildings lawn, and then signed too.

By the time the rains came there were Lumina clinics in every state capital, clean white buildings with clean white signs, and the queues outside them began at four in the morning.

One grey dawn, walking to school, Chidinma passed the new clinic on Adeniran Ogunsanya Street and saw a woman near the front of the queue, holding an umbrella and a brown envelope. It took her a moment to recognise Halima's mother.

The woman saw her too, and looked away.

Part Two: The Family Table

They will use your love as the rope to pull you, because they know you will never cut it. — one of Nne's sayings

Sunday lunch at Nne's house in Ikeja was a law older than any Accord. Pepper soup, then rice, then the arguments, in that order, under a ceiling fan that had turned the same slow circles since 1987.

Everyone was there. Nne at the head of the table, small and straight-backed, eighty-four years old. Mummy beside her, thinner than she had been a year ago, the dialysis port taped under her sleeve. Uncle Emeka, Mummy's elder brother, still wearing his red chieftaincy cap from a wedding that morning. Obinna, Chidinma's fiancé, polite and careful as he always was in this house. And Tobenna, Chidinma's brother, who had not touched his soup.

He said it the way you announce you have bought a car you cannot afford: quickly, looking at the bowl.

"I've booked my first appointment."

Nobody asked for what. Everybody knew for what.

Tobenna was thirty-one, with a first-class degree in petroleum engineering from UNILAG and a master's from Aberdeen that had cost their late father a plot of land in Nnewi. He had applied for two hundred and six jobs in four countries. He kept a spreadsheet with a column titled Reason Given, which was almost always empty.

"Houston," he said. "Final interview. The panel loved me. Then the HR woman asked if I had considered the Accord. Like it was a pension plan. They gave the job to a Clear Card guy from Kumasi with two years less experience."

"So they are blackmailing you," said Chidinma.

"They are offering me a door, Chi. You want me to stand outside it for principle? Principle doesn't pay for Mummy's dialysis."

Mummy put down her spoon. It made a very small sound, and the whole table heard it.

"Tobenna," she said. "Do not put my kidneys inside this matter."

"Mummy, I'm only saying—"

"I know what you are saying. You are saying that if you do this thing, it will be because of me. So that when you look in the mirror and see a stranger, you can say, I did it for my mother." Her voice did not rise, but it hardened. "If you want to do it, do it. You are a grown man. But carry your own load. I will not be the excuse you give to your ancestors."

Tobenna's jaw tightened. "Then who pays the clinic, Mummy? Who? Chi's teacher salary? Uncle's chieftaincy title?"

"Ehn, leave my title out of it," said Uncle Emeka, and then, because he could never resist a speech, he wiped his mouth and leaned forward. "But the boy is not completely wrong. Listen. In my father's time, Igbo men took English names to get work on the railway. Christopher, Benjamin, Augustine. Did we stop being Igbo? After the war they took everything from us and gave every family twenty pounds, no matter what was in the bank. Did we stop being Igbo? We traded. We built again. The Igbo man adapts. That is our genius."

"Uncle," Chidinma said, "you can take off an English name when you get home. You can never take off this."

"How do you know what can be taken off? Maybe next year they will make a serum to reverse it."

"And maybe next year they will make the Clear Card cost something else."

Obinna cleared his throat. He had said nothing all through the soup. "Chi, I think we shouldn't judge Tobe too quickly."

She turned to him. "Meaning what?"

"Meaning…" He chose the words the way a man chooses where to step in a dark room. "My office in Toronto has started asking too. Not officially. Just questions. Have you looked into it? Does your fiancée have plans? Everyone is asking, Chi. It's not just him."

The room cooled by several degrees.

"Does your fiancée have plans," Chidinma repeated slowly.

"I'm not saying anything. I'm just telling you what they asked."

Tobenna softened his voice, the way he used to when they were children and he had broken something of hers. "Chi. It's skin. It's just skin. I'll still be me. I'll still be Igbo. I'll still speak the language. I'll still come home every Christmas and eat Nne's ofe onugbu until I can't breathe."

All through it, Nne had said nothing. She only looked at her grandson for a long time, as though she were memorising his face.

Then she spoke for the first and only time that afternoon.

"Tobenna."

"Nne?"

"When you come home at Christmas, knock first. So that I will know it is you."

She rose from the table, took her walking stick from the wall, and went to her room. Nobody finished the rice.

Later, in the car, Obinna drove in silence until they reached the Third Mainland Bridge. The lagoon below was black and wide.

"I wasn't asking you to do anything," he said.

"I know," said Chidinma. "That's what frightens me. Nobody is ever asking."

Part Three: Lagos Speaks

Until the lion learns to tell the story, the tale of the hunt will always praise the hunter. — one of Nne's sayings

The country argued about Lumina the way it argued about everything: loudly, everywhere, and all at once. In the danfo buses, in the beer parlours, on every phone. A famous Afrobeats star posted a video of himself on a yacht in Dubai, freshly Clear, arms spread wide: They used to search my bag at every airport. Now they carry it for me. Eleven million likes.

But the argument everyone remembered happened on a Friday night, on Lagos Speaks.

Segun Oyelaran had two guests in the studio. On his left sat Professor Amaka Ezeugo, a historian from Nsukka with grey locks and reading glasses on a beaded chain, whose essay against the Accord had been shared four million times. On his right sat Mrs Bisola Hart, West Africa Director of Lumina Biotherapeutics, a former beauty queen, Clear for eight months, in a cream suit that made her look carved from soap.

"My dear Mrs Hart," Segun began, "the floor is yours. Tell Lagos why it should take your injection."

"Thank you, Segun. First, nobody has to take anything. Lumina is a choice. For the first time in history, African people can choose how the world receives them. We have been told for centuries to wait for racism to end. We are tired of waiting. Lumina says: stop waiting."

"And our culture?"

"Culture is not skin. Culture is language. Food. Music. Faith. Family. I still speak Yoruba with my mother. I still dance at owambe. Lumina changes how much pigment your skin cells make. It does not touch who you are."

Professor Ezeugo took off her glasses. "May I?"

"Please, Prof."

"Mrs Hart is right about one thing. Nobody is forced. That is precisely what makes it brilliant. The colonial administrators needed soldiers, missionaries and a flag. This needs only a discount on your mortgage. It is the most efficient colonial instrument ever designed, because it asks us to hold the whip ourselves and calls it freedom."

"That is very poetic, Professor—"

"Then here is something less poetic. Read the Accord. Not one clause asks the banks to stop discriminating. Not one clause asks the embassies, the police or the hiring managers to change. It asks only that the people who suffer the discrimination remove themselves from it. They did not fix the lock, Mrs Hart. They asked us to change the shape of our hands."

The board lit up like a Christmas tree.

The first caller was Iya Basira from Mushin, who sold pepper and onions at the market. "Prof, God bless you, but you are talking from university. My son took it. He is in Dubai now, driving for a big hotel. Every month he sends me money. Before, nobody would hire him. Culture did not feed me. My son fed me."

The second was a young man named Ifeanyi from Yaba, shouting so loudly Segun had to turn him down. "Our fathers fought for independence and we are queuing to become British again! For a visa! Shame on all of you!"

"My dear Ifeanyi," said Segun, "you are entitled to your anger, but not to my speakers. Next caller."

Then Segun leaned into his microphone, and his voice went quiet.

"Mrs Hart, before the next caller, a question from the host. I have been white since the day I was born. If white skin is the key, why have I been locked outside my whole life?"

Mrs Hart hesitated for the first time. "Mr Oyelaran, with the greatest respect, albinism is a medical condition. Lumina provides a complete, stable, healthy—"

"A complete what, madam? A complete white?" Segun took off his dark glasses and looked at her with his pale, light-sensitive eyes. "So the colour is not enough. It must be the right white, with the right card, approved by the right office. Then it was never about the skin, was it? It was always about who has the power to decide what a skin is worth."

The studio was silent. In ten thousand cars on the Lagos expressways, people turned up their radios.

The last caller was a woman who gave only her first name, Ngozi. Her voice shook.

"Mrs Hart. My husband and I are both Clear now. We are trying for a baby. Please tell me. If we take it, will our children be born white?"

A pause. A long one, for radio.

"The long-term effects across generations are still being studied," said Mrs Hart. "Our science team is very confident—"

"Still being studied," said Professor Ezeugo. "Write that down, Lagos."

---

On Monday, Chidinma's staff room replayed the whole programme.

"I am with Iya Basira," said Mr Adeyemi, who taught mathematics and had four children. "I cannot eat culture. My children cannot eat culture. If this card opens a door for them, I will take it and I will apologise to my ancestors later."

Mrs Bassey, head of English, slammed down her tea. "Apologise for what? For being practical? Our grandparents survived slave ships and colonial offices and three coups without bleaching themselves out of existence. And we will do it for a mortgage discount?"

"Our grandparents had no choice," Adeyemi said quietly. "We do. That is the cruelty of it. They have made it a choice, so that when it is done, they can say we chose."

Chidinma said nothing. But that night she lay awake, turning his words over like a coin. Force would have been resisted. Force would have been a crime. This was something slower, and much harder to fight. This was an incentive.

And somewhere in the city, a woman named Ngozi was lying awake too, with her hand on her stomach.

Part Four: Nne's Room

A people who do not know where the rain began to beat them cannot say where they dried their bodies. — one of Nne's sayings

Nne's room smelled of camphor, palm kernel oil and old paper.

She had been a midwife in Onitsha for forty years and had delivered, by her own count, three thousand two hundred babies, including half the current state assembly. She kept a ledger of every one: name, date, weight, the time of the first cry.

Chidinma came the week after the radio programme. She did not know what she wanted. Perhaps permission to be angry. Perhaps permission not to be.

Nne was sitting on her bed with a small calabash of uli dye on her knee, black and thick as ink.

"Give me your hand," she said.

Chidinma held it out. With a thin stick Nne drew on the back of it: a spiral, a line of small triangles, a curving shape like a bird's wing.

"This one is agwolagwo, the coiled snake. The circle that does not end. This one is onwa, the moon. This one has no name in English. My mother called it the thing that remembers."

"Nne, why are you doing this?"

"Because uli was made for dark skin. The dye is dark and the skin is dark, and the beauty is in how the two speak to each other. On white skin it looks like dirt. Like a bruise. Like a mistake." She set down the stick. "When I was a girl, the missionaries told us uli was heathen. Our names were heathen. My mother hid her calabash in the yam barn. Because of her, I can still draw this. Because of me, you can still wear it."

Chidinma looked at her painted hand. "Obinna's company is asking about me. Whether I have plans."

Nne grunted. "And Obinna? What are his plans?"

"He says he isn't asking me to do anything."

"A man who says he is not asking is already asking. He just wants the answer to be your idea."

Chidinma laughed despite herself, and then, suddenly, she was crying. "Nne, maybe Uncle Emeka is right. Maybe this is just survival. We bent for the missionaries. We bent for the British. We bent after the war. Maybe bending is what we do."

Nne was quiet for a long while. Outside, a hawker passed the gate calling gala, gala, cold mineral.

"Let me tell you about the war," she said. "In 1968 I was twenty-six, a young midwife in Umuahia. We had no salt. The children's bellies swelled with kwashiorkor and their hair turned red. The only thing keeping us breathing was the relief planes that landed at night on the airstrip at Uli."

"Uli? Like the dye?"

"The same name. The town gave its name to both, or maybe the dye gave its name to the town. Nobody remembers." A small smile. "One night a white man from the relief agency offered me a seat on the plane going out. He said I was young, I was trained, I could have a life in Lisbon or London. I only had to leave. Leave my mother. Leave my patients. Leave."

"What did you do?"

"I stayed. And I delivered nineteen babies that month, in a church with no roof, by the light of palm oil lamps. Eleven of them lived." Her eyes were wet, but her voice was steady. "Hear me, nwa m. There is a difference between bending so that you can stand again, and lying down so that others can step over you. Taking an English name to work on the railway is bending. You go home and your mother still calls you by your real name. But this thing…" She shook her head. "This thing asks you to lie down and never get up. And it asks your children to lie down with you."

"My children?"

"Ikenna's wife called me last week. Ngozi. She heard that woman on the radio say still being studied. She asked me, as a midwife, what will happen to a Clear woman's baby."

"What did you tell her?"

"I told her the truth. In forty years I have caught three thousand two hundred babies. Every single one came out wearing the face of some grandmother or great-grandfather. The body remembers, Chidinma. You can paint a wall. You cannot paint the foundation." She took Chidinma's painted hand in both of hers. "They are selling a coat and calling it a body. One day a child will come and take the coat off."

"So what do I tell Tobenna?"

"Tell him nothing. He has already decided." Nne pressed her thumb gently over the bird's wing. "You, I am telling. Someone must remember what we looked like. Someone must keep the calabash in the yam barn."

Part Five: The Clinic and the Ring

The one who truly loves the flower does not ask it to change its colour before he waters it. — one of Nne's sayings

Chidinma went with Tobenna to his third injection, because he asked her to, and because he was her brother.

The Lumina clinic on Awolowo Road had been a bank. The marble floors remained, but the walls were painted a white so bright it hurt. Soft flute music played. Every nurse had completed the course; their skin was pale and even and strangely without shine, like paper.

Tobenna was already lighter. After two doses his face had faded to the colour of weak tea. Only his lips held a little of their old darkness, and the nurse said that would go with the third.

In the waiting area Chidinma watched the others. A market woman holding hands with her two teenage daughters. A young man in a suit answering emails. An elderly couple sitting very straight, not speaking. And near the window, a woman she knew: Halima's mother, with Halima beside her in her school uniform, swinging her legs.

Halima saw her teacher and waved, delighted. Then she saw her mother's face, and stopped waving.

The mother came over. "Miss Okafor. Please. Don't look at me like that."

"I'm not looking at you like anything, ma."

"My husband's company is transferring him to Frankfurt. The family visa needs all of us Clear. All of us." She held the brown envelope against her chest like a shield. "You think I want this for her? You think I sleep?"

"Is it even safe for children?"

"They say they have a paediatric programme now. Gentle doses. Age-appropriate." She said the words the way people repeat a prayer in a language they do not understand.

Across the room, Halima was staring at the testimonial screen on the wall.

I got the job the same week.

The police don't stop me any more.

My daughter's teachers finally see her.

I feel free.

Chidinma noticed that nobody in the videos ever said I feel like myself.

When the nurse called Tobenna's name, he squeezed her shoulder. "Wait for me," he said, the way he used to when they were children and he ran ahead to buy puff-puff.

Forty minutes later he came out holding a small white card with a hologram of a sunrise. His face in the photo was the face she had known all her life, and it was also a stranger's. He turned it over in his fingers, and for a moment he looked like a man checking his pockets for something he had already lost.

Then his phone buzzed. His whole body changed.

"Houston," he whispered. "They want me to start in March."

---

That Saturday Obinna took her to dinner on Victoria Island, at a rooftop restaurant where the waiters were all Clear and the lagoon glittered below like spilled sequins. He was nervous. He kept adjusting his cufflinks.

Over dessert he took out a small velvet box.

"Chidinma Adaku Okafor," he said. "I have loved you since the first day you corrected my grammar at Yinka's party. Will you marry me? Properly. Church, traditional wedding, everything."

The ring was beautiful. She felt her heart rise toward it.

"And then Toronto," he went on, faster now. "They've offered me the permanent role. Senior analyst. A house, Chi, not a flat. A good school for our children. The only thing is the spouse visa. It's under the Harmonised Family category now, so…" He swallowed. "So we'd both need to be Clear. I've already booked my first dose. I wanted to do it first, so you'd know I'm not asking you to do anything I wouldn't do."

The lagoon kept glittering. The waiter kept not looking at them.

"Obinna," she said slowly. "Who are you proposing to? Me, or the woman I would have to become?"

"That's not fair."

"Isn't it? You want to marry me on the condition that I stop looking like me."

"I want to marry you on the condition that we can live, Chi! Do you know what Lagos will look like in five years? Every good job, every scholarship, every visa, Clear first. Your principle will not feed our children. Pride is a luxury."

"No," said Chidinma. "Pride is cheap. What I am talking about is expensive. It's the most expensive thing I own."

She closed the velvet box gently and slid it back across the table.

"I'm not saying no. I'm saying ask me again when you are asking for me."

She took a keke home alone. On the back of her hand, faded almost to nothing, she could still see the faint line of the bird's wing that Nne had drawn: the thing that remembers.

Part Six: The Babies

You can borrow another man's coat, but your children will still be born naked. — one of Nne's sayings

Ngozi's baby was born at Lagos Island Maternity at 4:12 on a Thursday morning: a boy, seven pounds, healthy, loud, and as dark as the inside of a cocoa pod.

Ngozi, pale as chalk on the delivery bed, wept with joy. Her husband Ikenna, Uncle Emeka's son, pale as chalk beside her, looked at the child for one long second and walked out of the ward without a word.

By evening the whole family had heard. By the next day they had gathered at Uncle Emeka's house in Lekki, in a sitting room full of gold-framed chieftaincy photographs, for what Uncle Emeka called "a family meeting" and everyone else understood to be a trial.

"Both of us are Clear," Ikenna said, standing, his voice shaking. "Both of us. So somebody explain to me where this black child came from."

Ngozi sat on the sofa with the baby, her face swollen from crying. "He is your son, Ikenna. I swear on my mother's grave."

"Your mother is alive!"

"Then I swear on mine," said a voice from the doorway.

Nne had come, against every doctor's advice, in a wheelchair pushed by Chidinma. She was frailer now, her breath short, but her eyes were as sharp as a razor.

"Bring the child to me."

Ngozi carried him over. Nne took him in hands that had held three thousand two hundred newborns and turned him gently toward the light.

"This is Emeka's nose," she said. "This forehead is my husband's, God rest him. These ears are your great-grandfather Okonkwo's; he could hear a lie from Onitsha to Enugu." She looked up at Ikenna. "You took a coat, my son. The coat did not go into your blood. Your blood has just greeted you."

"I want a DNA test," said Ikenna.

"Then do it," said Nne. "And when it comes back, you will kneel before your wife."

The test came back nine days later. Probability of paternity: 99.98 per cent.

Ikenna knelt.

---

They were not the only ones. Across the country, across the continent, the first wave of babies conceived after Lumina was arriving, and every single one was born in the colour of its ancestors.

In Ibadan a man threw his wife and newborn out at midnight; the neighbours took them in. In Accra a pastor declared the babies a sign of curse and was shouted out of his own church. In Nairobi a woman sued her husband for slander and won. The hashtag #LuminaBabies trended for eleven days. Paternity testing labs opened in every shopping mall.

Lumina Biotherapeutics held a press conference in a white hall in Abuja. Mrs Bisola Hart read from a prepared statement.

"As clearly stated on page forty-seven, clause nine, of every consent form: Lumina is a somatic treatment and does not alter the germline. Offspring of treated individuals will express their inherited phenotype. We deeply regret any distress caused by misunderstanding."

That night, on Lagos Speaks, Segun read the clause out loud three times, slowly.

"My dear people. They told us. They told us in small grey print on page forty-seven, in words most of us need a dictionary to read. Just as our great-grandfathers were told, in treaties written in a language they did not speak, that they had agreed to give away their land." He paused. "A truth told so that nobody will understand it is not honesty, my dears. It is a trap with a receipt."

---

Two weeks later the government announced the Family Continuity Protocol.

It was presented as a kindness. To keep a family's Clear benefits whole, including the parents' visa category, any child of Clear parents would begin a paediatric Lumina course at six months. Gentle, age-appropriate dosing, the brochure said. So your family can share one future.

On the first warm day of the new term, Halima came back to Chidinma's class.

She was pale now, the same flat, shineless pale as the nurses. She wore long sleeves in the heat. During break she did not go outside; she sat by the window with her arms wrapped round herself.

"Halima, won't you play?"

The girl rolled up one sleeve. Her forearm was red and blistered where the sun had caught it during sports day.

"Miss," she whispered. "The sun bites me now."

Chidinma called Segun Oyelaran that afternoon. She had never met him; she simply rang the station and kept ringing until they put her through. The next Monday he arrived at the school in his wide straw hat and dark glasses, carrying two cartons of sunscreen and forty sunhats donated by a Lagos albinism foundation.

He sat cross-legged on the classroom floor, and thirty-two children sat around him.

"My dears," he said, "I have been fighting the sun for forty-three years. Do you know what melanin is?"

Nobody did.

"It is the thing that makes your skin brown. And it is not decoration. It is armour. Your great-great-grandmothers lived under this sun for ten thousand years, and their bodies made armour for you, and passed it down, mother to child, like a family sword." He looked gently at Halima. "Some people have taken off their armour because they were told it was ugly. And now they need a hat like mine."

He put his own straw hat on Halima's head. It fell over her eyes, and for the first time in weeks, she laughed.

---

Nne died in the harmattan, quietly, in her sleep, with her ledger open on the bedside table. Her last entry was not a baby. It was a single line in her careful midwife's hand: The body remembers. Keep the calabash.

At the funeral in Onitsha, Chidinma counted. Of the forty-one relatives who came, twenty-six were Clear. Before the burial she sat in the compound with Nne's calabash and painted uli on the hands of anyone who asked. On the dark hands the patterns sang. On the pale hands they looked exactly as Nne had said: like bruises, like dirt, like a mistake. One of her aunties looked at her painted wrist and began to cry, and could not say why, and went to wash it off.

Ikenna came, with Ngozi and the baby. They had named him Nnaemeka, Father has done great things. Ikenna asked Chidinma to paint the baby's hand. As she drew the coiled snake on that tiny dark fist, he said, very quietly, "I'm stopping the protocol. They can take my visa category. I'm not putting that needle into my son."

Tobenna could not come. His employer had a policy against unscheduled travel in the first two years. He sent money. He sent a long voice note that Chidinma played three times. He sent photographs of his new wife Ifeoma, a Clear nurse from Enugu he had met in Houston, glowing and heavily pregnant.

The baby was born in April. The first message from Tobenna came at three in the morning, Lagos time.

Chi, she is dark.

A minute later: Nobody told us.

A minute after that: That's a lie. They told us. We just didn't want to read it.

He called the next evening. In the background the baby was crying, a thin, furious cry.

"The paediatrician brought up the protocol before Ifeoma had even been stitched," he said. "Like a vaccination schedule. And I just stood there holding her, Chi. She looks like you. She looks like Mummy. She looks like Nne." His voice broke. "I didn't know what my own face looked like any more, until I saw hers."

Part Seven: The Germline

When the roots of a tree begin to rot, the rot does not stop at the roots. — one of Nne's sayings

Dr Femi Lawal had worked in Lumina's Lagos laboratory for three years. He was a geneticist, Clear, careful, and the kind of man who folded his receipts before putting them in his wallet. He had never broken a rule in his life.

At eleven o'clock on a wet Wednesday night, he sat in his car in the empty car park of a shopping mall in Ikeja, waiting for a journalist.

Funmi Adeoye got into the passenger seat with rain in her hair. Since the Minister's interview, her programme had been pushed to one o'clock in the morning. Almost nobody watched it. That was why Femi had chosen her.

"You said you had documents," she said.

He handed her a flash drive. His hand was shaking.

"It's called Lumina Generation," he said. "The first serum only changes the skin. That's why the babies come back dark. Inside the company they call it phenotypic reversion, and they see it as the single greatest threat to the programme. Every dark baby is a walking advertisement that it doesn't work."

"So what is Generation?"

"A modified course for adults. It doesn't just change your skin. It changes your eggs or your sperm. Whatever you pass on." He swallowed. "Take Generation, and your children will be born Clear. And their children. And theirs. Forever."

Funmi stared at him.

"There's more. Amendment Two to the Accord. They want to create a new status, Tier One. Full benefits for life, inheritable, only for Generation families. Ordinary Clear people get downgraded to Tier Two. The children's protocol gets phased out, because, and I'm quoting the internal memo, Generation ends reversion within a single generation."

"Ends reversion," Funmi repeated slowly. "You mean it ends Black children being born."

Femi closed his eyes. "My wife is five months pregnant. I helped build this. I told myself I was building a door." He laughed, and it came out like a sob. "I was building a wall inside the womb."

---

Funmi aired the documents at one o'clock that morning. By six they had been viewed two million times. By noon, twenty million. By evening, the Senate had announced that it would vote on ratifying Amendment Two in five weeks.

The debate in the Senate chamber was broadcast live, and the whole country stopped to watch.

Senator Babatunde Kolawole, the Minister's closest ally, spoke first. "Distinguished colleagues, let us be practical. Our children will never again be stopped at a border. Never again followed in a shop. Never again passed over for a job. We are not erasing anyone. We are freeing generations not yet born from a burden we have carried for four hundred years."

Then Senator Hauwa Bello rose, a seventy-year-old former schoolteacher from Bauchi who rarely spoke.

"Distinguished colleagues. The first serum asked each of us to change ourselves. That was our own choice, our own body, our own sin or our own sense. But this," she held up the leaked memo, "this asks us to decide, on behalf of every child not yet born, that they must never look like their grandmothers. Who gave us that right? We are not their owners. We are their ancestors. And an ancestor's only duty is to hand down what was handed to her, whole."

The gallery rose to its feet. The Senate President banged his gavel for three full minutes.

---

The Calabash Movement began as a hashtag and became, within a week, a crowd.

Professor Ezeugo chose the name. "Because a grandmother once hid her calabash in a yam barn," she told reporters, "and because of her, we still remember."

On the last Saturday before the vote, two hundred thousand people filled Tafawa Balewa Square. Dark faces and Clear faces, side by side, many with uli patterns painted on their hands. Segun Oyelaran stood on the stage in his wide straw hat. Ikenna and Ngozi stood near the front with baby Nnaemeka on Ngozi's back. Mrs Bassey had brought the entire English department.

And beside her, to Chidinma's astonishment, stood Mr Adeyemi, pale as paper.

"I took it," he said simply, before she could ask. "For my children's school fees. That was my choice, and I will carry it. But I will not sell my grandchildren's bodies to pay for my mortgage. There is a line."

Iya Basira from Mushin was there too, in a bright blue headtie, holding a hand-painted sign: MY SON SENDS MONEY. I WANT A GRANDCHILD WHO LOOKS LIKE ME.

Professor Ezeugo spoke. Segun spoke. Then, because Segun had insisted, Chidinma was handed the microphone.

She had prepared a speech. She looked at the two hundred thousand faces and forgot every word of it.

"I'm a primary school teacher," she said. Her voice echoed back to her from the far end of the square. "On the day this started, a little girl in my class asked me a question. She asked, Miss, if I become white, will my grandmother still know me?"

The square went quiet.

"I didn't answer her. I told her to open her maths book. I've been ashamed of that ever since. So here is my answer, Halima, a year late." She took a breath. "Your grandmother will always know you. She will know your laugh and your voice and the way you hold your head. But if we let this law pass, your grandchildren will never know her. They will look at her photograph and see a stranger. They will look at the uli patterns and see dirt. They will have been decided before they were born."

She held up her painted hand.

"They told us it was only skin. It was never only skin. It was memory. And a people who give away their memory do not become free. They become available."

The roar that went up from Tafawa Balewa Square was heard, people said afterwards, all the way to Victoria Island.

In a hotel room in Houston, watching the livestream on his phone with his daughter asleep on his chest, Tobenna Okafor put his hand over his mouth and wept.

Part Eight: What Remains

A dawn is never white. It is the dark sky remembering the sun. — one of Nne's sayings

This story will not tell you how the Senate voted.

It doesn't matter as much as you think. That vote is held again in every generation, under a different name. Once it was called baptism, and the price was your name. Once it was called trade, and the price was a mirror and a bottle of gin for a man. Once it was called a cream, sold in Balogun Market, and the price was your mother's knuckles turning grey. Once it was called an accent, and the price was the way your grandmother said your name. Once it was called a visa queue, and the price was four in the morning for twenty years.

Lumina was only the newest name for the oldest offer in the world: Become a little less yourself, and we will let you in.

And the offer always comes with a light. Clean white walls. A sunrise hologram. A billboard that never goes dark. Whatever they sell us, they sell in the light.

What we keep, we keep in the dark. In a calabash. In a yam barn. In the blood.

---

Tobenna came home at Christmas.

It was the night before Christmas Eve, and the harmattan had painted Lagos the colour of old photographs. Chidinma was alone in Nne's room in Ikeja, which was hers now, with the camphor and the palm kernel oil and the open ledger, when she heard it.

A knock.

Then a second. Then a third.

She knew before she opened the door. Nobody in the family knocked. Doors in that house were for walking through, shouting Nne, we have come! Only one person had ever been told to knock.

He stood in the doorway, pale as the dust on the windows, with a small dark bundle asleep against his chest in a yellow knitted hat.

"It's me," said Tobenna. "I knocked. So she would know."

Chidinma could not speak. She only stepped back and let him in.

---

For a long time they sat on Nne's bed without talking. The baby, Nkechi Uloma, what God gave, a good home, slept on.

On the wall hung Nne's old mirror, so spotted with age that it no longer showed colour clearly, only shapes and shadows. Tobenna caught sight of himself in it with his daughter in his arms, and went very still. In that old glass there was no pale man and dark child. There was only one face, shown twice: once older, once new.

"The appointment was in January," he said at last. "For her. The paediatric course."

"Was?"

He reached into his jacket and took out the white card with its sunrise hologram. He turned it over in his fingers, the way he had at the clinic. Then he got up, went to the corner where Nne's calabash sat, and put the card inside it, beside the dark dye, and put the lid on.

"Nne said someone must keep the calabash in the yam barn," he said. "I want to keep this in it. So my daughter will see one day what her father nearly paid. And what he didn't."

He held out his own pale hand.

"Paint me."

"Tobe, on your skin it will look like—"

"Like a bruise. Like a wound. I know." His voice was steady. "Leave it. It is one."

So she painted him. The coiled snake, the circle that does not end. The moon. The bird's wing, the thing that remembers. On his hand the uli looked exactly as Nne had promised: like dirt, like a mistake, like something broken. He looked at it for a long time, and did not wash it off.

Then, very gently, so as not to wake her, Chidinma lifted Nkechi's tiny hand and drew the same three shapes on it.

On the baby's dark skin, the patterns sang.

Two hands, side by side. One wounded. One whole. The same patterns on both, drawn by the same hand, from the same calabash.

---

At midnight NEPA took the light, as NEPA always does at the worst possible moment.

The ceiling fan slowed and stopped. The house went silent. And out on the expressway, the great Lumina billboard, ONE WORLD. ONE FUTURE. LUMINA., the one that had burned white every night for three years, flickered once and went black.

In the sudden darkness, the stars came out over Lagos, faint through the harmattan haze. Somewhere a neighbour's generator coughed. Somewhere someone laughed. Somewhere a church choir kept singing without electricity, the way choirs had sung in a church with no roof in Umuahia in 1968.

In the dark, there was no Clear and no unClear. There were only voices. There was only the warmth of a sleeping child, and the smell of camphor, and two siblings sitting on their grandmother's bed.

Chidinma's phone glowed once. A message from Toronto.

It's Obinna. I watched your speech eleven times. I'm not asking for the woman you would become. I'm asking for you. Whenever you're ready.

She did not answer yet. Some answers need daylight.

---

They stayed awake until dawn.

And when it came, the dawn over Lagos was not white. It had never been white. It came up grey, then violet, then the deep red of uli dye, then gold, every colour at once, the whole dark sky slowly remembering the sun. The fair dawn they had all been promised never came, because no dawn was ever fair. Every dawn is the night, transformed and still itself.

Chidinma opened Nne's ledger to the next empty line, and in her own hand, beneath her grandmother's last words, she wrote:

Nkechi Uloma Okafor. Born in April. Weight: seven pounds, two ounces. Colour: her own.

And beneath it, the lesson Nne had spent her whole life teaching, which Chidinma had only now finished learning:

They will always offer you a new face. Every generation, a new name, a new light, a new door. The only answer is to remember the old face, and hand it down whole. Not because it is beautiful, although it is. Because it is ours. And a people who give away what is theirs do not become free. They only become easier to forget.

Nkechi stirred. She opened her eyes, looked up at her father, and wrapped her small dark hand around his pale, painted finger.

She held on with the fierce, astonishing strength of the newly born.

And she did not let go.

The End.
